# Plan around reduced capacity

## Core idea

Failures may add tasks while reducing visibility, mobility or communication. Do not assume a team can perform a complex response merely because it can describe it. Rehearsed skills, usable equipment and sufficient reserves are practical prerequisites. Use training-specific responses; this entry does not teach lost-line searches, valve isolation or gas-sharing execution.

## Original example

A dry briefing sounds fluent but the team has not practiced the proposed equipment response. That is a capability gap, not a wording problem.


## Check understanding

Explain the assumption most likely to make the example unsafe if ignored.

## Learning objective
Recognize an emerging loss of capacity before several problems combine.

## Understand the escalation
An extra task draws attention away from gas, position, route or teammates. An unresolved difficulty can increase effort and urgency, narrowing attention further. Repeating an unsuccessful action, missing familiar signals or abandoning normal checks can indicate overload. Such observations are prompts to assess the situation, not diagnoses of a teammate's mental state.

## Compare cases
One diver pauses a camera task when team awareness deteriorates. Another keeps adjusting the camera while missing signals. The equipment task is identical; the effect on the team's remaining capacity differs.

When breathing is available, gaining control, assessing the problem, communicating and reassessing the response can prevent escalation. An immediate threat to breathing requires the trained urgent response; a general reminder to pause must not delay restoring a breathable gas supply.

## Debrief
What changed first? What did each diver notice? Which task could have been removed earlier? What assumption concealed the growing workload? Use the answers to identify supervised practice needs.
